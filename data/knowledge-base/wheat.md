## Crop Stages

Wheat is a winter (rabi) cereal crop with well-defined growth stages: germination, seedling, tillering (side-shoot formation, which largely determines yield potential), jointing/stem elongation, booting, heading/flowering (anthesis), and grain filling through to physiological maturity. The crop cycle from sowing to harvest is typically 110-150 days depending on variety and sowing time. Timely sowing (within the recommended window for the region) is one of the strongest yield determinants, since late sowing pushes grain filling into hotter weather and reduces grain size.

## Soil Requirements

Wheat grows well on a wide range of soils but performs best on well-drained loamy to clay-loam soil with good moisture-retention capacity and a pH near neutral (6.0-7.5). Fields are typically prepared with 2-3 ploughings followed by planking to create a fine, firm seedbed, which improves germination uniformity. Standing water at any stage is harmful, particularly at germination and during grain filling.

## Irrigation

Wheat is typically grown with 4-6 irrigations depending on soil type, rainfall, and variety, with the most critical stages being crown root initiation (about 20-25 days after sowing), tillering, jointing, flowering, and grain filling — moisture stress at crown root initiation or grain filling causes the sharpest yield losses. In assured irrigation areas, irrigation scheduling is often tied to these physiological stages rather than a fixed calendar. In rain-fed areas, wheat relies more on stored soil moisture from monsoon rains, and variety choice/sowing time is adjusted accordingly.

## Fertilizers & Nutrients

Nitrogen is the dominant nutrient need for wheat and is usually split between basal application at sowing and one or two top-dressings at tillering and jointing, since a single large basal dose is prone to loss and less efficiently used. Phosphorus and potassium are typically applied fully at sowing to support early root development and later stress tolerance. Zinc deficiency is common in wheat grown on alkaline or heavily cropped soils and shows as interveinal chlorosis and stunted growth; sulfur deficiency shows as uniform yellowing of younger leaves, distinguishing it from nitrogen deficiency which affects older leaves first.

## Diseases

Rust diseases (yellow/stripe rust, brown/leaf rust, and black/stem rust) are the most significant wheat diseases globally, appearing as powdery orange, brown, or black pustules on leaves and stems; they spread rapidly under favorable humidity and temperature and can cause major yield loss if not managed through resistant varieties and timely monitoring. Powdery mildew shows as a white powdery coating on leaves, common in dense canopies with high humidity and moderate temperatures. Loose smut replaces grain in the ear with a mass of black spores and is managed primarily through seed treatment and use of certified disease-free seed, since it cannot be controlled once symptoms appear in the field.

## Pests & Pest Management

Aphids are the most common wheat pest, clustering on leaves and ears, causing direct feeding damage and honeydew that promotes sooty mold; natural predators such as ladybird beetles often keep populations in check if broad-spectrum spraying is avoided. Termites can damage roots and the base of stems, particularly in light soils and moisture-stressed crops, causing whitish, dried-up plants ("white ear head" symptom) that pull out easily. Armyworm and cutworm caterpillars can cause significant defoliation or cut seedlings at the base during outbreak years. Field monitoring, timely sowing, balanced fertilization (avoiding excess nitrogen which favors aphids), and conserving natural enemies form the basis of integrated pest management in wheat.

## Harvesting & Post-Harvest

Wheat is ready for harvest when the crop turns golden-yellow, grains harden, and moisture content drops to around 20-25% in the field (further reduced by drying before storage). Delayed harvesting increases the risk of grain shattering and losses from birds, rain, or lodging. After harvesting and threshing, grain should be dried to about 12% moisture content before storage to prevent fungal growth and insect infestation. Clean, dry, well-ventilated storage with protection from moisture and pests preserves grain quality and germination viability for seed use.
