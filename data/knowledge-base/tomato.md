## Crop Stages

Tomato is a short-duration warm-season crop, typically transplanted as 3-4 week-old nursery seedlings and reaching first harvest 60-90 days after transplanting depending on variety (determinate types finish sooner, indeterminate types keep flowering and fruiting over a longer window). Key stages are nursery/seedling, vegetative growth, flowering, fruit set, and fruit development/ripening, with fruiting often continuing over several weeks to months for indeterminate varieties under staking.

## Soil Requirements

Tomato grows best in well-drained sandy loam to loamy soil rich in organic matter, with a pH of 6.0-6.8. Good drainage is essential — waterlogged soil quickly leads to root diseases and poor fruit set. Raised beds are commonly used in heavier or poorly drained soils to improve aeration and reduce disease pressure. Incorporating well-rotted farmyard manure or compost before transplanting improves both structure and nutrient supply.

## Irrigation

Tomato needs consistent soil moisture, especially during flowering and fruit development — irregular watering (drought followed by heavy irrigation) is the main cause of blossom-end rot and fruit cracking. Drip irrigation is well suited to tomato as it keeps foliage dry (reducing fungal disease risk) and allows precise, frequent watering. Irrigation should be reduced slightly as fruit approaches full ripeness to concentrate flavor and reduce splitting, but never withheld to the point of wilting.

## Fertilizers & Nutrients

Tomato is a heavy feeder, requiring balanced nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, with potassium demand rising sharply during fruit development. Excess nitrogen late in the season promotes vegetative growth at the expense of fruiting and can worsen disease susceptibility. Calcium availability is critical — calcium deficiency (often triggered by inconsistent watering rather than lack of soil calcium) is the direct cause of blossom-end rot, a dark sunken patch at the fruit's blossom end. Magnesium deficiency shows as interveinal yellowing on older leaves. Split fertilizer applications across the season, adjusted for a soil test where possible, generally outperform a single large dose.

## Diseases

Early blight (Alternaria) causes concentric-ringed brown spots on older leaves first, while late blight (Phytophthora infestans) causes irregular water-soaked lesions that can rapidly destroy foliage and fruit in cool, wet weather — late blight can devastate a crop within days if unmanaged. Bacterial wilt causes sudden wilting of the whole plant without yellowing, often confirmed by a milky bacterial ooze from a cut stem placed in water. Tomato is also highly susceptible to viral diseases spread by whiteflies (such as leaf curl viruses), which cause upward curling, yellowing, and stunted growth with no direct cure — prevention via whitefly control and resistant varieties is key. General prevention includes crop rotation, resistant varieties, staking to improve airflow, avoiding overhead irrigation, and prompt removal of infected plants.

## Pests & Pest Management

Whiteflies are a major concern both for direct feeding damage and as vectors of viral diseases; yellow sticky traps and reflective mulches help monitor and reduce populations. Fruit borer (Helicoverpa) larvae bore into developing fruit, causing direct crop loss and entry points for rot; pheromone traps help monitor adult moth activity for timely intervention. Aphids cause curling and stunting of young growth and also transmit viruses. Leaf miners create visible winding trails inside leaves, reducing photosynthetic area. An integrated approach combining monitoring, biological control, and need-based rather than routine spraying is recommended to manage resistance and protect pollinators.

## Harvesting & Post-Harvest

Tomatoes for local/fresh markets are usually picked at the "breaker" to fully ripe stage depending on transport distance, while fruit destined for longer transport is often picked slightly under-ripe (mature green to breaker stage) and allowed to ripen in transit or storage. Harvesting should be done by hand, avoiding bruising, with fruit sorted by ripeness and size immediately after picking. Tomatoes are sensitive to chilling injury below about 10°C, so cold storage temperature should be managed carefully; short-term storage at 12-15°C with moderate humidity is generally recommended to slow ripening without chilling damage.
