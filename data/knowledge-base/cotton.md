## Crop Stages

Cotton is a long-duration warm-season crop (roughly 150-180 days) with stages of germination, seedling establishment, squaring (formation of flower buds called squares), flowering, boll formation, and boll opening/maturity. Because flowering and boll formation continue over an extended period, a cotton plant typically carries squares, flowers, and bolls of different ages simultaneously, which makes both nutrient management and pest monitoring an ongoing task rather than a single-window activity.

## Soil Requirements

Cotton grows best on deep, well-drained black soil (regur) or medium-textured loamy soils with good moisture-holding capacity, and a pH range of 6.0-8.0. It has a deep root system and moderate drought tolerance once established, but is sensitive to waterlogging, especially in the early stages, which causes root damage and stunted growth. Deep ploughing before sowing to break any hardpan improves root penetration and moisture retention.

## Irrigation

Cotton's critical water-sensitive stages are squaring, flowering, and boll development — moisture stress during these windows directly reduces boll number and size. Excess irrigation or waterlogging, especially during vegetative growth, encourages excessive vegetative growth at the expense of fruiting and increases disease pressure. In rain-fed cotton regions, sowing time is aligned with monsoon onset, while irrigated cotton commonly uses furrow or drip irrigation with scheduling tied to these critical growth stages rather than a fixed interval.

## Fertilizers & Nutrients

Cotton requires a balanced nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium program, with nitrogen typically split across basal, squaring, and flowering stages since excess early nitrogen promotes vegetative growth and delays maturity. Potassium is particularly important during boll development for fiber quality and boll retention. Micronutrient deficiencies of zinc, boron, and magnesium are common in intensively cropped cotton soils; boron deficiency in particular is linked to poor boll retention and shedding of squares and flowers.

## Diseases

Bacterial blight (angular leaf spot) causes angular, water-soaked lesions on leaves that turn dark brown, spreading rapidly in humid, rainy conditions and worsened by seed-borne infection — using treated, certified seed is a key preventive step. Fusarium wilt and Verticillium wilt are soil-borne fungal diseases causing yellowing, wilting, and vascular discoloration inside the stem, often more severe under water stress and in fields with a history of the disease; management relies heavily on resistant varieties and crop rotation since soil-borne inoculum is difficult to eliminate. Root rot, more common in hot weather and light soils, causes sudden wilting of scattered plants in patches.

## Pests & Pest Management

The pink bollworm is one of the most damaging cotton pests, with larvae boring into flower buds and bolls, causing internal damage often invisible from the outside until bolls are opened — pheromone traps for monitoring and timely destruction of crop residue after harvest are important control measures. Whiteflies cause direct feeding damage and transmit viral diseases, and also produce honeydew leading to sooty mold; they are typically managed with an integrated approach rather than routine spraying due to resistance concerns. Aphids and jassids (leafhoppers) damage young leaves, with jassid damage showing as leaf reddening/curling ("hopper burn"). Because cotton has a long fruiting window, regular field scouting through the season (not a single spray schedule) is central to effective integrated pest management.

## Harvesting & Post-Harvest

Cotton bolls are harvested (picked) as they naturally split open and the fiber fluffs out, which happens progressively over several weeks to months rather than all at once, requiring multiple picking rounds. Picking should be done in dry conditions and lint kept free from leaf trash, soil, and moisture to preserve fiber quality and grade. Seed cotton should be dried and stored in a clean, dry, well-ventilated area away from contamination before ginning, since moisture and trash content are major factors in the market price and quality grading of cotton lint.
