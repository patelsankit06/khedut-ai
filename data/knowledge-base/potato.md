## Crop Stages

Potato is grown from seed tubers rather than true seed, with stages of sprouting, vegetative growth (haulm/foliage development), tuber initiation (stolon tips begin swelling, typically 30-45 days after planting), tuber bulking (the main period of yield accumulation), and maturity/senescence when the foliage naturally yellows and dries. The crop is short-duration, typically 75-110 days depending on variety, and is highly sensitive to day length and temperature, generally preferring cool weather.

## Soil Requirements

Potato grows best in well-drained, loose, sandy loam soil rich in organic matter with a slightly acidic to neutral pH (5.5-6.5); loose soil structure is especially important because compacted or heavy clay soil restricts tuber expansion and causes misshapen tubers. Good drainage is critical, as waterlogging quickly causes tuber rot. Ridging or hilling soil around the base of plants as they grow is standard practice to keep developing tubers covered, preventing greening from sun exposure and protecting against some pests.

## Irrigation

Potato has a shallow root system and needs frequent, light irrigation to maintain consistently moist (not waterlogged) soil, particularly critical during tuber initiation and bulking. Irregular watering — especially a dry spell followed by heavy irrigation — is a common cause of tuber cracking and hollow heart. Irrigation is typically reduced or stopped as the crop approaches maturity (foliage yellowing) to allow the skin to set properly for storage, since harvesting from wet soil increases the risk of rot in storage.

## Fertilizers & Nutrients

Potato is a heavy feeder, especially for potassium, which strongly influences tuber size, starch content, and storage quality; nitrogen supports early canopy development but excess nitrogen late in the season delays maturity and can reduce tuber quality. Phosphorus supports early root and tuber initiation and is usually applied fully at planting. Calcium and magnesium deficiencies can contribute to internal tuber disorders. Because potato is planted from tubers placed directly in soil, fertilizer is often banded near the seed piece at planting with additional top-dressing during early bulking.

## Diseases

Late blight (Phytophthora infestans) is the most historically significant potato disease, causing dark, water-soaked lesions on leaves and stems that spread rapidly in cool, wet, humid conditions and can destroy a crop within days — this is the disease responsible for the 19th-century Irish potato famine and remains a major threat requiring vigilant monitoring, resistant varieties, and good field sanitation. Early blight (Alternaria) causes concentric brown "target-spot" lesions, usually on older leaves first, and progresses more slowly. Common scab causes rough, corky lesions on tuber skin (mostly cosmetic but reduces market value) and is worsened by alkaline soil and dry conditions during tuber initiation. Bacterial soft rot and blackleg cause tuber and stem rot, often introduced through wounds or contaminated seed tubers — using certified disease-free seed tubers is one of the most effective preventive measures.

## Pests & Pest Management

The potato tuber moth is a major pest, with larvae mining leaves and, more damagingly, boring into tubers both in the field (especially exposed tubers) and in storage — proper hilling to keep tubers covered and careful storage sanitation both reduce damage. Aphids cause direct feeding damage on foliage and, more importantly, transmit viral diseases that degrade seed tuber quality across generations, making aphid control especially important in seed potato production. Colorado potato beetle (where present) and cutworms can cause significant defoliation and seedling damage respectively. Crop rotation, certified seed, field sanitation, and monitoring-based intervention are the foundation of integrated pest management in potato.

## Harvesting & Post-Harvest

Potatoes are typically harvested after the foliage has naturally yellowed and died back, which allows the skin to "set" (thicken), improving storage life and reducing skinning damage during handling. Harvesting from wet or waterlogged soil should be avoided as it increases rot risk. After lifting, tubers should be cured in a well-ventilated, shaded area for a short period to heal minor wounds before storage. Potatoes store best in cool (around 4-10°C depending on end use), dark, well-ventilated conditions, since light exposure causes greening (which produces the bitter, toxic compound solanine) and warm storage accelerates sprouting and shrinkage.
