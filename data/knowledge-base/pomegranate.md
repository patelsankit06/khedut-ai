## Crop Stages

Pomegranate is a perennial shrub/small tree that typically starts bearing commercially from the 3rd year and reaches full production by year 5-6. The annual cycle after establishment has four broad stages: dormancy/pruning (post-harvest rest and canopy management), vegetative flush (new shoot growth following irrigation or rain), flowering (bahar), and fruit development (fruit set through maturity, roughly 5-7 months depending on variety and climate). Growers often manage three possible flowering seasons a year (Ambe/Mrig/Hasta bahar) but induce only one per plant per year by controlling irrigation and defoliation, since inducing more strains the plant and reduces fruit quality.

## Soil Requirements

Pomegranate tolerates a wide range of soils but performs best in deep, well-drained loamy soil with a pH between 6.5 and 7.5. It is notably tolerant of moderate salinity and drought compared to many fruit crops, which is why it is popular in semi-arid regions. However, it is sensitive to waterlogging — poor drainage is one of the most common causes of root rot and overall poor orchard health. Before planting, deep ploughing and organic matter incorporation (farmyard manure or compost) improve structure and water-holding capacity, especially in heavier soils.

## Irrigation

Young plants need frequent, light irrigation to establish a healthy root system; mature orchards are commonly irrigated via drip systems, which suit pomegranate's drought tolerance and salinity sensitivity to overwatering. Irrigation frequency should increase during fruit development, since irregular water supply (heavy irrigation after a dry spell) is the leading cause of fruit cracking. A general guideline is to maintain consistent, moderate soil moisture rather than allowing wide wet-dry cycles. Irrigation is deliberately withheld for 20-30 days before inducing a bahar (flowering) to stress the plant into flowering, then resumed gradually once flowering begins.

## Fertilizers & Nutrients

Pomegranate responds well to a balanced regime of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium (NPK) split across the growing season rather than applied all at once. Nitrogen supports vegetative flush and should be reduced as flowering approaches, since excess nitrogen at that stage promotes leafy growth over flowering and fruit set. Potassium is particularly important during flowering and fruit development, as it supports fruit size, color, and juice quality. Micronutrients most commonly deficient in pomegranate are iron, zinc, and boron; iron deficiency shows as interveinal yellowing (chlorosis) of younger leaves, while zinc deficiency causes small, narrow leaves. Farmyard manure or compost applied after harvest/pruning improves soil organic matter and nutrient availability. Exact fertilizer product choice and dose should follow a local soil test where possible.

## Diseases

The most economically damaging pomegranate disease is bacterial blight (caused by Xanthomonas), which shows as irregular dark brown to black water-soaked spots on leaves, fruit, and stems, sometimes with fruit cracking. It spreads rapidly in wet, humid weather and via contaminated tools, rain splash, or infected planting material. Other notable diseases include Cercospora fruit spot and Alternaria fruit rot (dark, sunken lesions on fruit, worse in humid conditions) and wilt (caused by soil-borne fungi such as Fusarium or Ceratocystis, showing as gradual yellowing and drying of the whole plant, often linked to poor drainage or nematode damage). Management is preventive: use disease-free planting material, avoid overhead irrigation, prune to improve air circulation, remove and destroy infected plant parts promptly, and sanitize pruning tools between plants.

## Pests & Pest Management

The pomegranate butterfly (fruit borer) is the most damaging pest — larvae bore into developing fruit, causing internal rot and premature dropping; affected fruit often show a small entry hole with gum exudation. Bagging young fruit with breathable covers is an effective non-chemical control. Thrips and mites attack young leaves and flowers, causing scarring and russeting on fruit skin, especially during hot, dry weather. Aphids and whiteflies can also build up on new flush and are often managed with need-based monitoring rather than routine spraying to preserve natural predators. An integrated approach — field sanitation, pheromone traps for fruit borer monitoring, encouraging natural enemies, and targeted intervention only when pest thresholds are crossed — is preferred over calendar-based spraying.

## Harvesting & Post-Harvest

Pomegranates do not ripen further after harvest, so timing is critical — fruit is picked when the skin develops full varietal color and produces a metallic sound when tapped, typically 5-7 months after fruit set. Harvesting is done by clipping (not pulling) the fruit stalk to avoid injury, which is a major entry point for post-harvest decay. Harvested fruit should be shade-cured briefly, sorted by size and blemish, and stored in a cool, well-ventilated place; refrigerated storage (around 5-7°C with moderate humidity) can extend shelf life to several weeks to a couple of months for export-quality fruit. Careful handling to avoid bruising and cracks is the single biggest factor in maintaining post-harvest quality.
